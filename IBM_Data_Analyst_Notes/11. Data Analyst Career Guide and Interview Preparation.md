## Key Concepts
- **Data Analyst** — acquires, integrates, organizes, cleans, validates, analyzes, and visualizes data; finds patterns; tells a coherent story to stakeholders. The job varies by **industry** and **function**.
- **Domain knowledge** — knowing how systems and processes work helps diagnose problems and validate results (e.g. medical-device pathologies; IoT failure modes on a manufacturing line).
- **Related titles** — do not let the job title alone deter you; read responsibilities. Analyst-titled roles (Operations, Social Media, Business, BI, Marketing, Financial) plus non-analyst titles (Data Scientist, Statistician, Actuary, and others listed below).
- **ATS (applicant tracking systems)** — software that scans incoming resumes. Prefer a **plain layout** and a **Microsoft Word (.doc)** copy; many templates and multi-column/image layouts are not ATS-readable.
- **XYZ formula / action–task–result** — resume bullets: action word + task + quantified result. Dialogue wording: “Accomplished X, as measured by Y, by doing Z.”
- **Elevator pitch** — short memorized statement answering **Who are you? What do you do? What do you want?** Used for networking, cover letters, and “tell us about yourself.”
- **Informational interview** — you interview a professional to learn about a field of work (industry research), not a hiring interview.
- **STAR** — Situation, Task, Action, Result (format for behavioral/situational answers).
- **Red flags in listings** — unrealistic requirements (e.g. years of experience for an entry-level role); broad expectations with few specifics.
- **Second-round types** — HR screen, team-leader/team interview, technical interview.
- **Culture fit** — how well a candidate fits existing company culture; can mask bias.

## Detailed Notes

### Week 1 — Build a Foundation

Your job search is more effective if you do preliminary work first: understand the jobs you want, write a resume, collect or create portfolio work, and prepare a cover letter and elevator pitch.

#### Data Analyst’s role
A Data Analyst acquires data, integrates and organizes it, cleans and validates it, and makes sense of it with statistical techniques. Analysts find patterns that help solve real-world problems. They model and visualize data to communicate insights to stakeholders. The job varies by industry and by function/department/division.

> [!note]
> Domain knowledge makes analysts more valuable. Example: medical-device data is easier to interpret if you understand the pathologies the team is detecting. Example: IoT data for manufacturing failures is easier to validate if you understand the process and how failures occur.

#### Industry sectors that employ Data Analysts
| Industry | Typical tasks |
|---|---|
| Financial services | Understand market trends; identify investment opportunities and risks |
| Retail | Analyze consumer behavior; optimize inventories; personalized experiences |
| Manufacturing | Optimize supply chain; improve operations and processes |
| Healthcare | Improve patient outcomes; optimize resources; reduce costs |
| Information Technology (IT) | Improve user experience; identify bugs; streamline data center operations |
| Government | Identify economic indicators and trends; support policy-making |

#### Functions that employ Data Analysts
| Function | Typical tasks |
|---|---|
| Marketing | Consumer behavior, target segments, campaign effectiveness |
| Sales | Competitive landscape, sales summaries, forecasts, prioritizing opportunities |
| Finance | Budgeting, financial performance, investment decisions, risk |
| Operations | Bottlenecks, process improvement, defects, inventory costs |
| Research & Development | Market research, evaluate research outcomes, analyze project data |
| Human Resources (HR) | Employee performance, reduce turnover, talent management |

Functional expertise makes interpretations actionable. Example: hospital-system protocols can help fill patient-data gaps in a clinical study. Example: knowing your company’s construction-cost process can identify avoidable costs and lower a bid.

#### Related job roles
Look for “Data Analyst” postings **and** similar roles. Core analytics skills alone do not qualify you for every title below; domain experience or extra skills may be required. Two categories:

**Related “Analyst” roles**

- **Operations Analyst** — uses data to evaluate efficiency of workflows, line production, supply chain, and customer service; recommends improvements.
- **Social Media Analyst** — social metrics for campaign success; engagement/web analytics; influence social-media policy. Tools named: Sprout Social, Hootsuite, Google Analytics.
- **Business Analyst (BA)** — data-driven decisions like a data analyst, but focused on reviewing and improving the business: interviewing team members, financial models and visualizations, training/coaching staff to implement new systems; evaluate functions/structures; recommend growth or change.
- **Financial Analyst** — increase profit or mitigate loss; attribute revenue variation to initiatives; budgeting and forecasting.
- **Business Intelligence (BI) Analyst** — hybrid of data analyst and business analyst. Uses marketing, sales, and app data to create visualizations and reports; recommend solutions; primary objectives are maximize profit or stimulate growth. Sometimes confused with BA.
- **Marketing Analyst** — advertisement data, social/email campaigns, blog posts, webinars; target audiences; site traffic; patterns across initiatives.

**Other related roles (titles without “Analyst”)**
Algorithm Developer · Analytics Consultant · Analytics Specialist · BI Developer · Data Interpreter · Data Processor · Data Scientist · Data Specialist · Data Visualization Specialist · Machine Learning Developer · Machine Learning Engineer · Statistician · Actuary.

The summary also names **Data Engineer** among related roles that may involve data analytics skills.

> [!tip]
> Don’t let the title of a job post deter you if the description is a good fit. Read roles and responsibilities, not only the heading.

#### Lab: Draft your resume
**Need:** word processor; past work/skills; an example job listing to practice tailoring.

**Keep in mind**
- Keep a **Microsoft Word (.doc)** copy (the format companies ask for most) and a **plain-text** copy (easier to paste into website fields).
- Rarely longer than **two pages**.
- Tailor using the example listing; you will do this for most applications.
- Highlight parts likely to change so you can find them later.

**Should you use a template?** Many templates look interesting but are **not readable by applicant tracking software**. If an employer screens with ATS, they may miss your resume. Draft in a **plainer format** first; you can paste into a template later.

**Exercise 1 — Contact information**
1. Open a new document.
2. Name at the top (usually centered, or flush left). Legal name, or a nickname if that is what you use professionally.
3. Contact: phone and email at least. Street address is not necessary; town and state can help. Check phone and email carefully. Email must look professional.
4. Links to social media, portfolio, or other professional sites if you use them for networking. Everything on those accounts should be something you want employers to see.

**Exercise 2 — Summary**
The top of page one is the most important part. A manager may look for only a few seconds.

1. Optional one-sentence who you are and what you want. Example: *Data analytics professional with three years experience seeking an entry-level position in the field of medicine and health care.*
2. Summarize experience and skills; customize later. Include field skills (e.g. machine learning) and tools/languages. Example from the lab: skilled at acquiring, integrating, organizing, cleaning, validating, and applying statistics; identifying patterns; Tableau and Power BI to model/visualize and tell a story; writing/editing technical materials for varied audiences; proficient with R and extensive Python.

**Exercise 3 — Professional experience**
1. Current or most recent role first: title, company, start/end dates. Quantify when possible.
2. Pattern: **action word → task → result (quantified if possible)**. Action words named: *Initiated, constructed, converted, deployed, led, designed.* Example: *“Deployed improved database design, which increased efficiency by 25% and saved 15% in storage.”*
3. Major tasks/duties, but stay aware of length; concentrate on the most important/relevant.
4. Reverse chronological order. If you have more than **8–10 years**, only list the most recent few years.

**Exercise 4 — Education**
1. School, degree, major; optional significant focuses or classwork. Dates can be useful but can also indicate age and lead to discrimination — you may leave dates out.
2. Certifications, standalone courses, and similar.

**Exercise 5 — Optional sections** (especially if new to the market)
- **Projects** — independent work; highlight skills used outside a professional setting; include links, especially if in the portfolio.
- Conferences, professional publications, awards if relevant.
- Hobbies, interests, or volunteer work **only if directly relevant** to the position.

**Resume lab tips**
- Leave off a **photo** (may lead to discrimination) and **references** (provide separately when asked; they may change by job).
- Have a trusted colleague proofread; some employers drop candidates for mistakes.
- Save a copy in cloud storage (Google Docs or Dropbox) so you can find it from any device.

#### Lab: Create your basic cover letter
**Need:** word processor; past work (a resume helps); an example job listing.

**Keep in mind:** Word + plain-text copies; treat it as a template; highlight parts that will change (e.g. job title). Pen and paper works, but electronic is easier to edit.

**Exercise 1 — Open**
Opening such as *Dear (manager):* — replace the placeholder with the real contact name.

**Exercise 2 — First paragraph**
State the job title and why you might be a good fit. Mention where you saw the listing or who referred you. Express enthusiasm for the company. End with a brief self-introduction that transitions to the body.

Example: *I was excited when my colleague Janna Navarro told me your company had an opening for a Data Analyst. I have admired the products developed by Torotoni Software for many years, and I would be very interested in joining your team.* Then: *I am a data analyst with 3 years of professional experience and skills that match your needs, and I think I would be a strong candidate.*

**Exercise 3 — Main body**
Build a case: cite a few experiences that match the listing; draw parallels to resume and portfolio; quantify; keep brief; save some work to discuss in the interview.

Example: *In my most recent role, I maintained and extended a mission-critical database and prepared a weekly report of changes and trends for stakeholders. I also worked with the coding and management teams to improve functionality of the database by 45%, advised on compliance issues, and supervised the work of two interns. I know SQL and Python, have intermediate proficiency in Hadoop and Apache Spark, and have used Tableau software for visualization.*

**Exercise 4 — Close**
Restate interest and hope to meet; call to action; polite closing and full name.

Example: *Thank you for your consideration. I would love to bring my skills to Torotoni Software, and I'm eager to discuss the contributions I can make to your team. Please call me at your earliest convenience.* — *Best regards,* — *Olivia Torvald*

**Cover-letter lab tips:** proofread; reuse for email, post, or web paste; if sending by post, format as a proper business letter (address blocks — consult an outside source); save in cloud storage.

#### Gathering references
Collect references **ahead of time** and confirm they are willing to speak. For each person gather: name, title, company, mailing address, phone, email.

**Example**
Janice Thompson · Development Project Lead · Infinite Square Inc. · 1387 West 14th Street, Suite 4 · San Mateo, CA 94401 · 650-477-0822 Available between 3:00 to 5:00 PST · jthompson@infinitesquare.com

Do not use references without permission. Keep a directory you can find easily.

**Professional references** — people who can comment on past work, usually managers. Company policy may limit them to verifying employment and dates only — check so you know what they can share. Employers prefer supervisors, but you can also list: colleagues who worked closely with you; professors familiar with classwork if work history is short; people who supervised or worked with you on a volunteer project.

**Personal references** — character, trustworthiness, honesty, work ethic. Rarely needed in modern interviews, but gather some. People who know you well **and are not family**: friends, teachers, classmates, counselors. Avoid people who might reveal inappropriate personal information (e.g. clergy revealing religious affiliation). Get contact info and permission.

#### Drafting an elevator pitch
A short, easily memorized statement of yourself, key features as a job seeker, and goals. Employers rarely ask for it by name, but it helps with cover letters, networking, and “tell us about yourself.” It should answer three questions: **Who are you? What do you do? What do you want?**

**Who:** full name (and preferred name if you have one); handshake if face-to-face; career title and current situation.

**What:** a few key skills or experiences that set you apart — not the whole resume. Arrange them as a **story**, not a bullet list.

**Want:** an action request — interview, interest in a role, or whether your statement covered their question. Encourage engagement. Prepare a few possible endings; they depend on context.

Use natural language, avoid jargon, stay professional, use your own phrasing, and make it understandable by many people. Memorize; practice slowly and clearly (nerves speed you up). Practice with a friend. Write it to be broadly useful, then tailor (request or highlighted experience) as needed.

The interview-prep lab restates the pitch as about **three or four sentences**: name and title, recent experience, what you are looking for.

#### Portfolios and resumes (practice + graded themes)
A portfolio is especially valuable because it lets employers **examine projects that demonstrate your skills**. It does not replace a resume.

**Good portfolio examples:** freelance or internship projects that show practical problem solving; course or capstone projects from meaningful datasets; documented participation in data challenges such as **Kaggle**. Certificates alone, personal photos, and unrelated social media are not good portfolio content.

**Building a project:** use a **real-world public dataset**, do exploratory analysis, and create visualizations — not “describe a problem with no analysis,” not “only polished sample data that needs no cleaning,” and not “avoid public data because employers only want proprietary data.”

**GitHub:** host **several** projects, **update them often**, add a **README**. Organize public repos by skill or project type. Do not leave projects unchanged, dump everything in one undescribed repo, or keep strong work private.

When experience is limited, **relevant hobbies** can go on the resume (e.g. a sports-dashboard hobby as a project, highlighting tools and analysis — not a bare “interests” line).

Tailor: for two roles (e.g. e-commerce marketing analytics vs hospital operations), **emphasize the projects, metrics, and examples that match each role’s domain**. A recruiter’s ~15-second scan wants a clear skills section, relevant experience, and evidence that the portfolio supports the role.

### Week 2 — Applying and Preparing to Interview

This module is about being a memorable candidate: research companies, assess job leads, rehearse interviews, and network.

#### Applying for a job
Each company has its own process. Read carefully and **follow the directions exactly** or the application may be disqualified before a hiring manager sees it.

First, see whether they prefer an online application (example named: LinkedIn), a resume and cover letter to an email, or another procedure.

There is generally **no limit** to applications across different companies. Some companies **limit** applications in a time period — be strategic and choose the most interesting roles.

If you are interested in **multiple positions at one company**, it is usually better to **focus on one application** first (the role you are most qualified for or most interested in), then ask HR or a hiring manager whether to apply to each job or avoid duplicates. They can say whether an application can be forwarded or whether a profile lets you apply to multiple jobs easily.

#### Resume and cover letter checks (before you send)
**Resume — watch for:** outdated or missing contact information; grammatical and formatting errors; unprofessional email; cliché vocabulary or jargon; unrelated job experience or irrelevant hobbies; a photo of yourself (should not be on the resume).

**Cover letter — watch for:** grammar/formatting errors; too long (multiple pages) or too brief; wrong person or misspelled name; not tailored to company and opening; arrogant or too humble tone (aim professional and confident); listing references in the letter; explaining why you left a previous employer or other issues better discussed in an interview.

A cover letter is a **one-time first impression**. In **one page**, show qualifications and career aspirations so recruiters want to learn more and see whether your goals align with the company’s.

#### Other application materials
Employers may also request transcripts, writing samples, portfolios, certifications, a reference list, and letters of recommendation. Follow all instructions.

- **List of references** — do not provide too early if not requested; notify references they may be contacted; keep contact information correct.
- **Letters of recommendation** — avoid vague/generic content; lack of information that validates your work; letters that express reservations unless balanced with positive qualities.
- **School/college transcripts** — do not send your own copy if the company said a **sealed copy must go directly from the institution**.
- **Online portfolio URL** — verify the URL, that the link works, and that samples are recent and documented.
- **Work or writing samples** — grammar; shows your work effectively; avoid clichés/buzzwords; not too brief or too lengthy.
- **Certifications (trainings, licenses, others)** — valid signatures, seals, or logos; expiration still current; only relevant certificates.

#### Document naming and organization
Consistent naming; include your **full name**. If the resume is `John_Smith-Resume.pdf`, use `John_Smith-Cover_Letter.pdf`, and so on. If sorted alphabetically, all of your files should stay together.

Example set: `John_Smith-Cover_Letter.pdf` · `John_Smith-References.pdf` · `John_Smith-Resume.pdf` · `John_Smith-Transcripts.pdf`

Limit special characters to **underscores and/or hyphens**. If you use blank spaces, use them consistently. **Don’t include version numbers** in submitted file names. Submit **all requested documents at once**.

Cover-letter template: include name, job title, contact information; highlight customized parts; save with “template” in the name; when tailored, replace “template” with company or job title.

#### Researching companies and assessing listings (from course quizzes)
On a company website, **repeated keywords** show what matters to them and the image they want to project; you can even reuse some of those words in an interview. Formality can hint at culture; jargon can hint they are current — the graded item emphasizes **repeated keywords**.

Check **social media** to understand image and corporate culture from company and employee posts (current issues and culture). Do not treat it as a place to broadcast your personal life to employers.

**Red flags:** unrealistic requirements (e.g. several years’ experience for an entry-level role — they may not understand the role or may be exploitative); **broad expectations with few specifics**. A good listing defines the position clearly, with tasks that match the role and level. “No salary listed” and “no recruiter name” were **not** the red-flag answers in the practice quiz.

**Qualifications:** you do **not** need every listed qualification — have the required ones and many preferred ones. Qualifications may include **hard and soft skills**. They often **do** include years of experience (the false statement was that they often will not).

**Contractor vs full-time employee (FTE):** both usually work **full-time hours**. Most companies **don’t provide benefits to contractors** (they may get them from their agencies). They are not generally covered by the same employment laws; contractors should not expect to work indefinitely.

**LinkedIn** is the top networking website in this course; **almost 90%** of recruiters use it (feedback: **about 87%**).

**Effective networking methods (graded):** join professional groups and attend events; take classes (meet peers or instructors); talk to friends about your job search (even people outside the field may know someone). Talking to people at bars and clubs was **not** selected as effective here.

#### Lab: Prepare for an interview
**Need:** your elevator pitch; word processor. Knowing information is not the same as presenting it smoothly — rehearse.

**Exercise 1 — Practice the pitch**
Retrieve the pitch; keep it short and informative; memorize; practice until you can recall it. Lab example: *“My name is Angela Menendez and I'm a data scientist. For the past two years I've worked in an entry-level role helping derive business insights from data using established models at an e-commerce company. I would eventually like to move into designing machine learning models and I'm looking for a mid-level position with opportunities to work toward that goal.”*

**Exercise 2 — Prepare answers for common questions**
Copy the questions; use your resume for situations; write professional, complete, not overly long answers; read them aloud and reword anything hard to say.

**Sample interview questions**
- What attracted you to the field of data science?
- Can you recall a time when you had very little time to complete a task? How did you approach getting it done?
- Tell me about a time you disagreed with a coworker, and how you resolved the conflict.
- What was your favorite previous project? Why did you like it?
- Can you remember a time you disagreed with your supervisor? How did you solve the problem?
- Describe a situation where you made a mistake, and what you did to resolve the effects.

**Exercise 3 — Questions for interviewers**
Being asked if you have questions: **prepare some**. Saying no may seem polite; showing you have thought about the company makes a better impression. Be careful asking about **benefits and compensation too early** — leave that for offer negotiation. Practice saying questions aloud so you can listen to answers. Add company-specific questions from research. Ask for clarification when something comes up live.

**Example questions for interviewers**
- What is the structure of the team I would be joining, and what is its work style?
- What is the advancement path for this position in the company?
- What do you personally like about working for this company?
- How would you describe the organization's culture?
- How do you evaluate success in this role?

**Take it further:** rehearse in a mirror (habits/expressions); record audio and video; mock interview with a friend (including unprepared questions). Save notes in cloud storage.

**Rehearsal (quiz):** you cannot predict every question, but you **can** practice articulating what you want to say. A **mock interview** is a practice interview at home (e.g. a friend role-playing), not a company “doesn’t count” interview. For first interviews, **compose questions you want to ask** from your notes — do not decide salary/benefits first, memorize everything, or hand out a written answer sheet.

### Week 3 — Interviewing

Follow through after you have a company’s attention: interview types (including code challenges), impression in a final interview, and follow-up.

Interview processes **vary** by position type and company size. Entry-level interviews often differ from higher-level ones.

#### Unethical questions and behaviors
Laws and practices differ by place and country. Research your own area. This reading is general, with several **United States** examples. In nearly every case employers are ethical; if they ask the wrong questions you are **not obliged** to answer. Options: **deflect** toward the role/qualifications, or say you **don’t wish to answer** and move on. If that costs you the role, you may not want to work there.

**Personal information interviewers should not ask**
- **Race or nationality** — strictly illegal in the US and many other places. Watch indirect questions (name origin, first language). Citizenship/work-visa questions related to hiring can be allowable: **not OK** to ask point-blank whether a candidate is a citizen; **can be permissible** to ask whether they are legally allowed to work in the country.
- **Gender and sexual preference** — illegal to discriminate (and to ask) in the US.
- **Marital status and family plans** — illegal to ask marital status in the US; usually a proxy for “dedication.” Family plans may probe leave/insurance for women. Asking about a spouse is an invasion of privacy.
- **Health history** (including family) — unethical unless a rare job-related reason; often about insurance/sick time.
- **Disability** — may ask whether the candidate can perform the job if there are specific physical requirements; US employers must make **reasonable accommodations**. Unethical to ask about on-the-job injury or worker compensation.
- **Age** — OK to ask if they are of **legal age** for jobs where law defines eligibility. General age questions when irrelevant are not ethical. Indirect: asking **when you graduated**.
- **Legal or arrest record** — unethical to ask in the interview. If they have a legitimate reason, there are official channels. (Graded quiz: this was **not** grouped with the items marked **illegal in the US**; race/nationality, gender/sexual orientation, and marital status were.)
- **Financial history** — some employers may do a credit check **with permission when hired**; unethical to ask in the interview about credit, bank accounts, bankruptcy. Indirect: home ownership.

**Other employers / employment**
- **Company information** — do not ask for internal information beyond your projects/tasks (structure, other projects). Answering may violate NDAs.
- **Coworkers and managers** — checking supplied references is reasonable; asking for inside information about people you worked with is not.
- **Compensation** — until a solid offer, be careful discussing previous salaries. Focus on what they are offering or general market salary. Past salary can perpetuate unfair pay and invite a lowball.

**Behavior**
- **Religion** — illegal to ask in the US. Indirect: where you worship, weekends, weekend hours.
- **Testing** — required/relevant tests (e.g. urine) may exist, but it is unethical for an interviewer to ask whether you would be willing and decide hiring on that. Same for lie detector and genetic tests.
- **Use of substances** — should not ask about smoking, medications, alcohol, or other substance use on your own time. If drug testing is required, notify and test **after** a job is accepted, with permission.
- **Political opinions** — inappropriate; also unethical to question views on unions.
- **Personal appearance** — dress standards are OK to discuss; commenting/asking about appearance is not (can probe socio-economic status, gender, or discriminate on religion/race, e.g. hairstyles).
- **“Culture fit”** — be careful; it often masks preferring people who already work there (e.g. white, male, younger, or American).

#### Second-round screening
An invitation means first-round interviewers thought you might be a good fit. Second-round questions go deeper on **competencies, ethics, and outlook**, and whether you can work in the culture. Align answers with **mission and role**. The company has an opening because they need to grow or optimize a process — show how you contribute.

**Prepare from the first round**
- What went well — reuse it.
- Topics that got the most interest — prepare follow-up questions.
- Questions you would answer differently or that caught you off guard — prepare a better response.
- Names and job positions of people you met.
- Thoughts/questions/concerns that came up afterward.

(Graded: “whether you are still interested in the position” was **not** a listed review item.)

Second-round interviews are usually **more focused**. You may meet the same people, but more often **team leaders, technical managers, HR**, or others. You may meet executives. You may see any mix of the types below — not necessarily all.

**HR screen**
May even be a **first** interview. Basic questions about past experience and understanding of the **company and culture**. Not the nuts and bolts; **no involved technical questions**.

Example HR questions: What motivated you to apply? What attracted you to the listing? Why did you leave your last job? What is your ideal working environment? What do you know about our product or services? What are your expectations about salary and other compensation?

Relate answers to experience; stay positive. Frame a negative departure **neutrally and professionally**. **Don’t relate specific compensation history**; be candid about expected rates and flexibility.

**Team leader** (often a project manager)
Team you’d join and how they approach projects. Some skills discussion, but less than a technical screen. **Work style** and **behavioral** questions.

Example questions: first things you would do in the role; best practices in data analysis; a project you’d want to work on there; conflict with a colleague; a project that failed — what you did and learned; prefer close team vs mostly alone; how you are motivated; how you start learning an unknown topic.

Build rapport; use names if you’ve met them. Project as an already-hired teammate; discuss what you’d learn and accomplish; grow while excelling in the role.

Unusual questions (e.g. *“If you could have a superpower, which would it be and why?”*) may test stress, creativity, or problem approach. Answer honestly; have fun with them.

**Technical screen**
Usually a team leader or higher-level data analyst. Assess technical skills vs the role. Mix of technical and team-skill questions, plus **problems to solve**. They will **not** expect a perfect solution at once — they want your **approach and thinking**.

Example questions: coding languages you know; favorite data-related tools; how you begin an assigned problem; what you do when you don’t understand something; how you stay on schedule; how you prepare for a presentation.

**End every second interview positively:** explicit interest; thanks; smile, handshake, wish they find the right fit; ask for contact info; send requested materials (e.g. portfolio links) promptly; **thank-you notes the same day**; follow up if you haven’t heard back within the estimated timeframe.

#### Coding challenges, NDAs, STAR, final interviews (from quizzes)
**Coding challenges** vary in scope and complexity by **seniority and job description** (easier for entry-level; harder for senior). Typically you work **on your own time at home** and submit work; the company sends information, links, and a submission method. Usually **not** the first step. Visualizations may be part of the work (the false claim was that you will not need them). Instructions **are** provided (the false claim was that you must figure it out with no instructions).

**NDA** before a challenge: keep you from sharing problems with other applicants and establish **who owns the work**. Graded restatement: they do not want the problem shared with other potential candidates.

**STAR** for behavioral/situational answers: **Situation, Task, Action, Result**.

**Final interview:** an appropriate question is **“What’s the next step in the hiring process?”** — not asking for a higher salary there, time off, or “what does this company do?” Prepare by **testing equipment** if virtual. Asking interviewers questions shows you are serious and prepared — not the time to negotiate compensation or be candid about workload/holiday concerns. Don’t skip notes, leave appearance to the morning, or skip breakfast to save time.

#### After the interview
Send a thank-you note, then reflect. Track company, interview date, people met, outcome, questions asked, and whether you would answer differently. The job of finding a job isn’t over until you secure a position.

**If you get an offer**
You do **not** have to accept immediately. You can say you need time to review. Recruiters may suggest a timeframe and invite questions about the package. Generally decide within **two business days** — they may move on.

If salary or benefits are not fair, research the market, bring selling points, and request fair compensation. Weigh **benefits**: signing bonuses, moving allowances, stock options, other non-salary pay, commute, flexible hours.

Negotiate by phone or email: excitement and appreciation; how you will contribute; counteroffer based on **verifiable research** or supporting arguments. Legitimate arguments named: commute and relocation expenses; a university degree that wasn’t required but will help you exceed expectations; a skillset or certifications that took extensive/costly training, especially if they were **preferred** qualifications. Thank them for considering the request even if budget or pay-scale policy blocks it.

If you accept while still in process elsewhere, **withdraw** other applications courteously, ask them to keep your information, and protect your reputation.

If resigning, give **standard two weeks’ notice** when possible. Try to arrange a start date with **some time off** between jobs.

**If you are declined**
Learn from it; it is not a personal reflection — only one person is hired. Thank them for the notification (many companies never contact rejected applicants). **Request feedback** and keep an open mind. Many companies **prohibit** interview feedback for legal reasons. If you get feedback, reflect from application packet through rehearsal, then plan: better written answers, more rehearsal, training, a book, or coaching.

## Definitions
- **ATS (applicant tracking systems / applicant tracking software)** — tools that scan resumes; they work best with simple layouts and often with Microsoft Word files. Keywords should appear in normal readable text, not inside graphics.
- **XYZ formula** — “Accomplished X, as measured by Y, by doing Z.” Same idea as the resume lab’s action + task + quantified result.
- **Elevator pitch** — 3–4 sentence introduction: who you are, what you have done recently, what you want.
- **Informational interview** — you interview a professional to learn about a field.
- **Mock interview** — practice interview (e.g. with a friend) to rehearse pitch and answers at home.
- **STAR** — Situation, Task, Action, Result.
- **FTE** — full-time employee (contrasted with contractor).
- **NDA** — non-disclosure agreement (often before a coding challenge).
- **BI analyst** — hybrid of data analyst and business analyst; visualizations/reports from business data to maximize profit or stimulate growth.
- **Business analyst (BA)** — improve the business via interviews, models, visualizations, training; not the same as BI analyst.
- **Culture fit** — how well a candidate fits the company’s existing culture; can mask bias.
- **Red flag (listing)** — warning sign such as unrealistic requirements or vague, overly broad expectations.

- Data analyst work **varies by industry, function, and company size/context** — it is not only large tech companies and not the same tasks everywhere.

## Common Mistakes
> [!warning]
> Creative multi-column resumes, images, icons, and skills hidden in graphics — ATS may misread or miss you.

> [!warning]
> Photo on the resume; references on the resume or in the cover letter; unprofessional email; explaining why you left a job in the letter.

> [!warning]
> Sending a portfolio URL that is wrong, broken, or full of undocumented old work.

> [!warning]
> Providing references too early, with stale contact info, or without permission; listing a relative who has not seen your work.

> [!warning]
> Applying to several roles at one company without asking HR whether that is allowed.

> [!warning]
> Saying you have no questions for interviewers; asking about pay/time off too early or in a final interview instead of process/next steps.

> [!warning]
> Answering unethical questions under pressure; sharing past salary before an offer; treating “culture fit” as a neutral signal.

> [!warning]
> Accepting an offer immediately without weighing benefits, or ghosting other companies after you accept.

## Interview Questions

### Beginner
1. **Q: What is the primary role of a data analyst?**
   A: Gather, analyze, and communicate data insights to support business decisions — acquire, clean/validate, find patterns, visualize, and tell the story to stakeholders.
2. **Q: What three questions should an elevator pitch answer?**
   A: Who are you? What do you do? What do you want? (Introduce yourself, recent work, professional goals / ask.)
3. **Q: What belongs in the contact block of a resume, and what should you leave off?**
   A: Name, phone, professional email; town/state optional; portfolio/professional links. No street address required. No photo. No references on the resume.
4. **Q: What is an informational interview?**
   A: You interview a professional to learn about a field of work — industry research, not a company’s hiring interview.
5. **Q: Why keep a Microsoft Word resume with a simple layout?**
   A: Many ATS tools scan Word files more reliably; columns, images, and graphics can hide skills from the parser.

### Intermediate
1. **Q: How do a data analyst, a business analyst, and a BI analyst differ in this course?**
   A: Data analyst: acquire–clean–analyze–visualize–communicate. BA: improve the business via interviews, models, training, recommendations. BI analyst: hybrid; marketing/sales/app data into viz/reports to maximize profit or growth.
2. **Q: How should you write a resume bullet (XYZ / action–task–result)?**
   A: Action word + task + measurable result. Example: “Built interactive Tableau dashboards for marketing, reducing manual reporting time by 40%.” Dialogue form: accomplished X, as measured by Y, by doing Z.
3. **Q: What are listing red flags, and do you need every qualification?**
   A: Unrealistic requirements; broad expectations with few specifics. Have required qualifications and many preferred ones — you do not need every item. Hard and soft skills both appear.
4. **Q: Name strong questions to ask interviewers, and what to avoid early.**
   A: Team structure/work style; advancement path; what they like about the company; culture; how success is evaluated. Avoid benefits/compensation until offer stage; in a final interview, “What’s the next step?” is appropriate — not salary or time-off haggling.
5. **Q: What should you do if asked an unethical or illegal question?**
   A: You are not obliged to answer. Deflect toward qualifications/the role, or say you don’t wish to answer. US examples of illegal asks: race/nationality, gender/sexual orientation, marital status.

### Advanced
1. **Q: You are applying to e-commerce marketing analytics and hospital operations analytics. How do you tailor?**
   A: Same core skills, different emphasis: lead with the projects, metrics, and business questions that match each domain. Don’t use one generic packet or strip domain work to look “broader.”
2. **Q: How do you prepare differently for HR, team-leader, and technical second rounds?**
   A: HR: motivation, culture, products/services, why you left (neutral), salary expectations without past-pay history — no deep tech. Team leader: first 90-day actions, conflict, failed project, work style, motivation, learning approach; build rapport. Technical: languages/tools, how you start a problem, gaps, time, presentations, plus unsolved problems to show thinking — not a perfect solution on the spot.
3. **Q: Walk through offer vs decline follow-up.**
   A: Offer: don’t accept on the spot; ~two business days; weigh benefits; negotiate with research (commute/relocation, extra degree, costly preferred certs); if you accept, withdraw other applications and give two weeks’ notice; try for rest days before start. Decline: thank them, request feedback (they may be prohibited from giving it), then improve packet and rehearsal.
4. **Q: What GitHub setup survives a 15-second hiring-manager look?**
   A: Several **public** repos by skill/project, recently updated, README on each explaining the work. Not one junk drawer, not screenshots-only, not private “professional” repos.
5. **Q: How do STAR and “culture fit” show up in later interviews?**
   A: Use STAR (Situation, Task, Action, Result) for behavioral stories (conflict, failure, tight deadline, mistake). Treat culture-fit questions carefully — they can mask preferring people who already work there.
